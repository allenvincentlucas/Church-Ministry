Day 15 · 15 min

Introducing barre chords: why they're hard, and how to start

A barre chord uses one finger to press down multiple strings at once, acting like a moveable "capo" built into your hand. They're harder because they demand more finger strength and precise placement — but they also let you play any chord, anywhere on the neck, without needing a capo.

  • Practice pressing your index finger flat across all 6 strings at the 1st fret, checking each string rings clean one at a time
  • Don't expect a clean barre in one sitting — this is a strength-building exercise as much as a technique one
Barre chords take real finger strength to build — buzzing strings at first is normal, not a sign you're doing it wrong. Give it 2–3 weeks of daily practice before judging your progress.

The F chord shortcut

F is usually the first full barre chord beginners meet, and it can be discouraging. A simplified "mini-barre" version, using just two fingers, gives you a usable F chord right away while your full barre strength develops.

Day 17 · 20 min

Fingerpicking basics: the P-I-M-A pattern

Fingerpicking uses your thumb (P) and three fingers (I, M, A) to pluck individual strings instead of strumming across them — giving you a softer, more intimate texture that's common in worship intros and bridges.

  • Assign P (thumb) to the lowest string of a chord, and I, M, A to the next three strings up
  • Pluck in the order P-I-M-A slowly on a G chord, keeping a steady, even rhythm
  • Repeat on C and D once G feels controlled

Day 18 · 15 min

Alternate picking for speed and accuracy

Alternate picking (strictly down-up-down-up with a pick, note by note) is the foundation for faster lead lines and riffs. It trains your picking hand to stay efficient instead of picking every note in the same direction.

  • Pick a single string, alternating down-up-down-up at a slow, even tempo
  • Move across all 6 strings, keeping the alternating motion unbroken
  • Gradually increase tempo only once every note sounds identical in volume
Day 19 · 15 min

Reading a chord chart vs. tab vs. sheet music

Worship teams typically hand out one of three formats. Knowing what each one tells you — and doesn't — means you can walk into any rehearsal prepared.

FormatShows youDoesn't show you
Chord chartChord names over lyricsExact rhythm or fingering
Tab (tablature)Exact frets and strings to playRhythm, unless annotated
Sheet musicPrecise pitch and rhythmGuitar-specific fingering
Most worship charts (including everything in the Worship Chord Library) use the chord-chart format — it's the fastest to read live and the most common in church settings.
Day 20 · 15 min

Fixing a tense fretting hand

As barre chords get introduced, many players start gripping the neck with their thumb wrapped too far over the top, which limits reach and adds strain. A relaxed thumb, positioned behind the neck, gives your fingers more freedom.

  • Check your thumb position: it should sit roughly behind your first or second finger, not wrapped over the top
  • Shake out your fretting hand every few minutes during barre chord practice
  • If a barre chord buzzes, check your thumb and wrist angle before pressing harder
