She Won't Take Silence for an Answer

Jesus doesn't say a word to her at first. Then his disciples want her sent away. Then his own answer sounds like a refusal. She keeps going anyway — and turns his own words into her argument.

Matthew 15:21-28

📖 Today's Gospel

And Jesus went from thence, and retired into the coasts of Tyre and Sidon. And behold a woman of Canaan who came out of those coasts, crying out, said to him: Have mercy on me, O Lord, thou son of David: my daughter is grievously troubled by a devil. Who answered her not a word.

And his disciples came and besought him, saying: Send her away, for she crieth after us. And he answering, said: I was not sent but to the sheep, that are lost of the house of Israel. But she came and adored him, saying: Lord, help me. Who answering, said: It is not good to take the bread of the children, and to cast it to the dogs.

But she said: Yea, Lord; for the whelps also eat of the crumbs that fall from the table of their masters. Then Jesus answering, said to her: O woman, great is thy faith: be it done to thee as thou wilt. And her daughter was cured from that hour.

❤ Reflection

Notice how many times this woman could reasonably have stopped. First, Jesus says nothing at all — not a word, not even a "no." Silence is its own kind of hard answer. Then his own disciples want him to send her away, annoyed that she keeps calling out. Then Jesus himself finally speaks, and what he says sounds, on the surface, like a door closing: he was sent to Israel, not to her; it isn't right to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs. By any normal read, that's three separate signals to give up.

She doesn't take the metaphor as an insult and walk away offended. She takes it seriously and argues inside it: "even the dogs eat the scraps that fall from their masters' table." She isn't demanding to be treated as a child of the house — she's asking for exactly what's actually available to her, and trusting that it will be enough. Jesus doesn't rebuke her for pushing back. He says the opposite: "O woman, great is your faith." Her daughter is healed in that same hour.

Picture this: you've asked God for something — healing, provision, a change in someone you love — and the answer so far has just been quiet. No clear yes, no clear no. Maybe someone around you has even suggested it's time to stop bringing it up. This woman's example isn't "keep demanding until God gives in." It's staying honestly, humbly present in the asking, even through silence, even through an answer that stings, because you still believe the person you're asking can say yes.

Where have you gone quiet in prayer after what felt like a "no" — and what would it look like to keep showing up honestly, the way this woman did?

✝ Catechism Cross-References

CCC 2610

The Catechism points to Jesus's own teaching on prayer of petition as a call to "the boldness of faith" — asking without holding back, trusting the one being asked. This woman's persistence in the face of silence and a hard-sounding answer is exactly that kind of bold, trusting prayer in action.

Read CCC 2610 →
CCC 60

The Catechism describes the New Covenant as opening what was first given to Israel to "all peoples," Gentiles included, called into it in equal measure. A Canaanite woman receiving what Jesus first describes as being "for the lost sheep of the house of Israel" is that promise showing up inside a single conversation.

Read CCC 60 →
CCC 2777, 2828

On persistent, confident petition — approaching God the way a child asks a parent, honestly and without pretense, trusting the relationship even when the answer isn't immediate. That trust, not the eloquence of the request, is what the Catechism holds up as the heart of Christian prayer.

🙏 Closing Prayer

Lord, when your answer feels like silence, or like a no I don't understand, give me this woman's honest persistence — not demanding, not walking away offended, but staying at the door, trusting that you can still say yes. Amen.
