It Worked Before. Why Not This Time?

The disciples had cast out demons before — Jesus had already given them that authority. This time, nothing. His answer isn't about the size of their faith.

Matthew 17:14-20

📖 Today's Gospel

And when he was come to the multitude, there came to him a man falling down on his knees before him, saying: Lord, have pity on my son, for he is a lunatic, and suffereth much: for he falleth often into the fire, and often into the water. And I brought him to thy disciples, and they could not cure him.

Then Jesus answered and said: O unbelieving and perverse generation, how long shall I be with you? How long shall I suffer you? Bring him hither to me. And Jesus rebuked him, and the devil went out of him, and the child was cured from that hour.

Then came the disciples to Jesus secretly, and said: Why could not we cast him out? Jesus said to them: Because of your unbelief. For amen I say to you, if you have faith as a grain of mustard seed, you shall say to this mountain, Remove from hence hither, and it shall remove; and nothing shall be impossible to you.

❤ Reflection

The father's exhaustion is right there in how he describes it: his son falls into fire, falls into water, over and over. This has clearly been going on a long time, and he's already tried the obvious next step — Jesus's own disciples — and they couldn't help him. What makes their failure so striking is that this wasn't unfamiliar ground for them. Earlier in this same Gospel, Jesus had specifically given the Twelve authority to drive out unclean spirits. This wasn't their first time doing this. And yet, here, nothing happened.

Jesus's diagnosis is "little faith," which sounds at first like he's scolding them for not believing hard enough — as if the fix were simply to want it more. But he immediately undercuts that reading: faith the size of a mustard seed, the smallest seed there is, is enough to move a mountain. The problem was never the quantity of faith available to them. It looks more like complacency — treating faith as a skill they'd already mastered once, rather than something that has to be actively, presently trusted every single time they're asked to use it.

Picture this: a prayer practice, a ministry task, a way of trusting God that used to come naturally — and lately it just isn't landing the way it used to. The temptation is to assume something's fundamentally broken, or that you need a bigger breakthrough of faith to fix it. This Gospel suggests something smaller and more honest: maybe you've been coasting on old momentum instead of staying actively present in the trust itself.

Where has something that used to work in your spiritual life started to feel stalled — and could the issue be less about the size of your faith, and more about whether you've stayed actively present in it?

✝ Catechism Cross-References

CCC 162

The Catechism describes faith as something that must be "nourished" and "lived out" continually — it can grow, but it can also be weakened or lost if it isn't fed. The disciples' failure here isn't a one-time verdict on their character; it's a reminder that faith isn't a resource you stockpile once and coast on.

Read CCC 162 →
CCC 2610

Jesus's teaching on prayer and faith is described as a call to "the boldness of faith" — real, present trust, not a formula performed correctly once. That boldness is exactly what the mustard seed image points to: small but alive, not large but dormant.

Read CCC 2610 →
CCC 274

"Nothing is impossible with God" — the Catechism names this as one of the deepest grounds of Christian faith and hope, echoing exactly what Jesus tells his disciples here. The power was never limited by the size of their faith; it was always available in the God they were trusting.

🙏 Closing Prayer

Lord, when something that used to come easily now feels stalled, keep me from assuming my faith is too small. Help me stay actively, presently trusting you today, not coasting on what worked yesterday. Amen.
