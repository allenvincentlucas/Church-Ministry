What Looks Like Loss From the Outside

A grain of wheat has to disappear before it can become anything more than itself. Today's feast of a martyr makes that abstract image concrete.

John 12:24-26

📖 Today's Gospel

Amen, amen I say to you, unless the grain of wheat falling into the ground die, itself remaineth alone. But if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit. He that loveth his life, shall lose it; and he that hateth his life in this world, keepeth it unto life eternal.

If any man minister to me, let him follow me; and where I am, there also shall my minister be. If any man minister to me, him will my Father honour.

This page uses the Douay-Rheims (Challoner) translation, which is in the public domain. The official Mass reading for today uses the NABRE translation — you can read it at USCCB.org. ❤ Reflection

From outside, a grain of wheat falling into the ground and dying looks exactly like destruction — the seed disappears, breaks down, is gone. Jesus picks this image deliberately because it's counterintuitive: what looks like an ending is actually the only way the seed becomes anything more than itself. Left alone in a jar, it stays a single grain forever. Buried and lost, it becomes a harvest.

Today's feast makes that abstract image concrete. Lawrence was a deacon of Rome, entrusted with the Church's resources for the poor. Ordered to hand over the Church's treasures, he gathered the poor and the sick he served and told the prefect, "These are the Church's treasures." He was executed for it. From outside, on the day it happened, that looked like Rome winning and the Church losing one of its own. "Whoever loves his life loses it, and whoever hates his life in this world will preserve it for eternal life" isn't a call to recklessness — it's the same seed logic applied to an entire life, and Lawrence is what it looks like carried all the way through.

Picture this: most days won't ask for anything as dramatic as Lawrence's. But the same principle shows up in small, ordinary "dyings" — giving up being right in an argument, spending real time and energy on someone with nothing to give back, releasing a plan because following God costs your comfort instead. None of it looks like gain in the moment. All of it is the same seed, buried.

What's one place this week where holding on tightly has actually been keeping something from bearing fruit — and what would it look like to let it go?

✝ Catechism Cross-References

CCC 618

The Catechism describes the Cross as Christ's unique sacrifice, which he invites every disciple to share by taking up their own cross and following him. Lawrence's martyrdom is this teaching lived out to its furthest point, not a separate or unusual kind of holiness.

Read CCC 618 →
CCC 2015

"The way of perfection passes by way of the Cross. There is no holiness without renunciation and spiritual battle." The grain of wheat isn't a poetic exception to how holiness works — the Catechism names this dying-to-live pattern as the ordinary road every disciple travels.

Read CCC 2015 →
CCC 2446

On the Church's teaching regarding almsgiving and love for the poor — directly connected to Lawrence's specific ministry as a deacon entrusted with the Church's care for those in need, the very thing that led to his martyrdom.

🙏 Closing Prayer

Lord, show me the small dyings you're asking of me today — the grip I need to loosen, the comfort I need to release. Give me Lawrence's joy in letting go, trusting that what looks like loss from the outside is how you make a life bear fruit. Amen.
