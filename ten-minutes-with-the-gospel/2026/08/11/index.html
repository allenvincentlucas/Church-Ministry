The Answer Was a Child, Not an Argument

Who is the greatest? The disciples wanted a ranking. Jesus doesn't answer their question at all — he puts a child in the middle of them and says: become like this.

Matthew 18:1-5, 10, 12-14

📖 Today's Gospel

At that hour the disciples came to Jesus, saying: Who, thinkest thou, is the greater in the kingdom of heaven? And Jesus calling unto him a little child, set him in the midst of them, and said: Amen I say to you, unless you be converted, and become as little children, you shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven. Whosoever therefore shall humble himself as this little child, he is the greater in the kingdom of heaven.

See that you despise not one of these little ones: for I say to you, that their angels in heaven always see the face of my Father who is in heaven.

What think you? If a man have an hundred sheep, and one of them should go astray: doth he not leave the ninety-nine in the mountains, and go to seek that which is gone astray? And if it so be that he find it: amen I say to you, he rejoiceth more for that, than for the ninety-nine that went not astray. Even so it is not the will of your Father, who is in heaven, that one of these little ones should perish.

❤ Reflection

The disciples' question was competitive by nature. "Who is the greatest" assumes a ranking — someone coming out ahead, someone else behind. Jesus doesn't answer it on their terms at all. He doesn't name a winner or describe an achievement worth chasing. He simply calls a child over, stands them in the middle of the group, and says: this. Become like this.

A child in that culture had no status to leverage and no argument to make for their own importance — they simply received what was given to them. The passage moves straight from there into the parable of the lost sheep: the same God who holds up childlike smallness as the model of greatness is also the one who leaves the ninety-nine to go after the one that wandered off, because that one, insignificant by any competitive measure, still matters completely. Saint Clare, whose feast falls today, lived this same logic out — she gave up wealth and status to follow Francis into deliberate poverty, choosing smallness rather than having it forced on her.

Picture this: a conversation, a ministry role, or a moment in prayer where some part of you is quietly angling to be seen as capable, needed, important — negotiating for a better position instead of simply receiving what's offered. This Gospel doesn't ask you to perform smallness. It asks you to actually stop negotiating and just receive, the way a child does.

Where are you still negotiating for status instead of simply receiving, like a child — and what would it look like to let that go and become small?

✝ Catechism Cross-References

CCC 526

"To become children of God we must be 'born from above' or 'born of God.' Only when Christ is formed in us will the mystery of Christmas be fulfilled in us... becoming a child in relation to God is the condition for entering the kingdom." The childlikeness Jesus asks for here isn't a phase to outgrow — it's the actual shape of a life turned toward God.

CCC 336

On the ministry of angels, including guardian angels — directly connected to Jesus's statement that the "little ones'" angels "always look upon the face of my Father." Even the smallest, most overlooked person is personally attended to in heaven.

Read CCC 336 →
CCC 605

On God's universal will to save, and his refusal to lose even one — "he affirms that he came 'to give his life as a ransom for many'... it excludes no one." The lost sheep in today's parable is this teaching told as a story instead of a doctrine.
